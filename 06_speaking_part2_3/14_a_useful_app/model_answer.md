# Model answer — A Useful App or Website

## Part 2 · Model talk (about 2 minutes)

The app I'd like to talk about is a flashcard app that uses something called spaced repetition to help you memorise information. I've been using it for about a year now.

I found out about it from a colleague who was learning Japanese. She'd been using it every day and was amazed at how much vocabulary she'd remembered, so I decided to try it when I started preparing for IELTS.

The way it works is quite clever. You create digital flashcards — for example, with a word on one side and its meaning and an example sentence on the other. When you review a card, you tell the app whether you found it easy or difficult. Cards you find difficult appear again soon, while ones you know well are shown less and less frequently. I mainly use it for academic vocabulary and collocations, and I review my cards for about fifteen minutes every day on the train to work.

The reason it's so useful is that it focuses my time on exactly what I'm about to forget, which is much more efficient than rereading a long word list. It also turns studying into a small daily habit rather than something I have to find a big block of time for. Before I started using it, I'd learn new words and forget most of them within a week. Now I can recall most of the vocabulary I've studied, and I've started noticing myself using some of those words naturally in conversation.

So for me, it's been one of the most useful tools in my exam preparation.

## Part 3 · Model answers

**What are the most popular apps in your country?**

> Messaging apps are probably the most popular, followed by payment apps, which have become essential for everyday shopping. Food-delivery and ride-hailing apps are also widely used in cities.

**Do people rely too much on apps?**

> I think many people do. We use apps for navigation, payments and even reminders to drink water, so people may lose basic skills, and they can feel helpless when their phone isn't working.

**How might apps change in the future?**

> They'll probably become more personalised, using artificial intelligence to predict what we need before we ask. That could be convenient, but it also raises concerns about privacy.

## How to do Part 2 well
- **1 minute prep:** write 4–6 keywords, one per bullet point. Don't write sentences.
- **Speak for the full 2 minutes.** Cover every bullet, but spend longest on the last one ("explain why…") — it's where you show range.
- **Structure:** introduce → tell the story with past tenses → reflect on why it matters.
- **Signposting:** *I'd like to talk about…; What I remember most is…; The reason… is that…; Looking back…*
- **Part 3** is more abstract: give an answer, a reason, an example, and if possible a contrasting point (*That said…, On the other hand…*).
