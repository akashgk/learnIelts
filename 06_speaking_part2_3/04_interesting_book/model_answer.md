# Model answer — An Interesting Book

## Part 2 · Model talk (about 2 minutes)

The book I'd like to talk about is Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari, a history of humankind. I read it last year while I was on holiday, and I remember being so absorbed that I finished it in about a week, which is quick for me.

It's basically a story of how our species developed, from small groups of hunter-gatherers to the global, technological society we live in today. The author divides human history into a few major turning points — for example, the development of language, the beginning of farming and the scientific revolution — and explains how each one changed the way people lived.

What I found most interesting was that it made me question things I'd always taken for granted. For instance, the author argues that the agricultural revolution wasn't necessarily good for ordinary people, because farmers often worked harder and had a less varied diet than hunter-gatherers. I'd always assumed farming was simply progress, so that was eye-opening.

Another thing I liked was the style. It covers huge, complex ideas, but it's written in a very accessible way, with lots of everyday examples and a bit of humour. You don't need to be a historian to follow it.

Not everyone agrees with all of its arguments, and some experts have criticised it for oversimplifying. But for me, that's part of what made it interesting — it got me thinking and discussing the ideas with friends long after I'd finished reading.

## Part 3 · Model answers

**Why do some people prefer reading books to watching films?**

> Books allow you to use your imagination, because you create the characters and places in your own mind. They also go into much more depth — you can understand what characters are thinking, which is difficult to show on screen.

**Do you think e-books will completely replace printed books?**

> I doubt it, at least not in the near future. E-books are convenient, but many people still love the physical experience of a printed book, and research suggests some readers remember information better on paper. I think the two formats will continue to exist side by side.

**How can parents encourage children to read more?**

> The most effective way is probably to read with them and in front of them, so reading becomes a normal, enjoyable activity. Letting children choose their own books, even comics, also helps, because forcing them to read things they find boring can put them off.

## How to do Part 2 well
- **1 minute prep:** write 4–6 keywords, one per bullet point. Don't write sentences.
- **Speak for the full 2 minutes.** Cover every bullet, but spend longest on the last one ("explain why…") — it's where you show range.
- **Structure:** introduce → tell the story with past tenses → reflect on why it matters.
- **Signposting:** *I'd like to talk about…; What I remember most is…; The reason… is that…; Looking back…*
- **Part 3** is more abstract: give an answer, a reason, an example, and if possible a contrasting point (*That said…, On the other hand…*).
