# Model answer — Useful Advice You Received

## Part 2 · Model talk (about 2 minutes)

The best advice I've ever received came from my uncle, who's a civil engineer with over thirty years of experience. He gave it to me just before I started my first full-time job.

I was quite anxious at the time, because I was worried about looking inexperienced in front of my new colleagues. When I mentioned this to my uncle, he told me: 'In your first six months, always ask the stupid question.' He explained that new employees often waste hours trying to figure things out on their own because they're afraid of looking foolish, when a thirty-second question could solve the problem.

I took his advice quite literally. Whenever I didn't understand an abbreviation in a meeting, or wasn't sure how a system worked, I asked. At first it felt uncomfortable, but I quickly realised that most of my colleagues were happy to help, and some even admitted they didn't know the answers themselves.

I think it was good advice for several reasons. Firstly, it saved me a huge amount of time. Secondly, it helped me build relationships, because asking for help is actually a good way to get to know people. And thirdly, I learned much faster than I would have done otherwise.

Now that I've been working for a few years, I've passed the same advice on to new members of my team. I think it applies to almost any situation where you're learning something new, including preparing for exams like IELTS.

## Part 3 · Model answers

**Who do young people usually ask for advice?**

> It depends on the topic. For personal issues, many young people turn to friends of their own age, while for career or financial decisions they're more likely to ask parents or teachers. Increasingly, they also look for advice online.

**Is it a good idea to get advice from the internet?**

> It can be useful for practical information, but you have to be careful about the source. A lot of online advice is based on personal opinion rather than expertise, and it doesn't take your individual situation into account.

**Why do some people find it difficult to accept advice?**

> Some people see advice as criticism, or they feel it threatens their independence. Others have already made up their minds and only want someone to agree with them.

## How to do Part 2 well
- **1 minute prep:** write 4–6 keywords, one per bullet point. Don't write sentences.
- **Speak for the full 2 minutes.** Cover every bullet, but spend longest on the last one ("explain why…") — it's where you show range.
- **Structure:** introduce → tell the story with past tenses → reflect on why it matters.
- **Signposting:** *I'd like to talk about…; What I remember most is…; The reason… is that…; Looking back…*
- **Part 3** is more abstract: give an answer, a reason, an example, and if possible a contrasting point (*That said…, On the other hand…*).
