# Model answer — A Difficult Decision

## Part 2 · Model talk (about 2 minutes)

One of the most difficult decisions I've had to make was whether to accept a job offer in another city, about three years ago.

At the time, I was working for a small company in my hometown, living with my parents and earning a reasonable salary. Then I was offered a position at a large technology company in Bangalore, which is around five hundred kilometres away. The new job paid considerably more and offered much better career prospects, but accepting it meant leaving my family and friends behind and living alone for the first time.

I had two clear options. I could stay where I was, which was comfortable and safe, or I could move, which was exciting but also quite frightening. My father had recently had some health problems, so I was also worried about not being close by if my family needed me.

To make the decision, I did something quite simple: I wrote down the advantages and disadvantages of each option. Seeing everything on paper made it clearer. Then I talked to my parents honestly about my concerns. To my surprise, they were the ones who encouraged me most strongly to go. My father said he'd regret it more if I turned down the opportunity because of him.

In the end, I accepted the job. It was hard at first, but it turned out to be the right decision — I've learned a great deal, and I visit home every couple of months. It also taught me that talking openly with people involved can make a difficult decision much easier.

## Part 3 · Model answers

**What decisions do young people find difficult?**

> Choosing what to study and which career to pursue are probably the hardest, because they can affect the rest of your life. Deciding where to live and whether to move abroad are also big decisions.

**Should parents make decisions for their children?**

> For young children, yes, parents need to make most decisions. But as children grow older, I think parents should guide rather than decide, so that young people learn to take responsibility for their choices.

**Do people make better decisions alone or in groups?**

> Groups can bring different perspectives and spot problems an individual might miss. On the other hand, groups can take a long time and sometimes people just go along with the majority, so it depends on the type of decision.

## Useful phrases

- *career prospects*
- *leaving … behind*
- *seeing everything on paper*
- *turned down the opportunity*
- *go along with the majority*

## How to do Part 2 well
- **1 minute prep:** write 4–6 keywords, one per bullet point. Don't write sentences.
- **Speak for the full 2 minutes.** Cover every bullet, but spend longest on the last one ("explain why…") — it's where you show range.
- **Structure:** introduce → tell the story with past tenses → reflect on why it matters.
- **Signposting:** *I'd like to talk about…; What I remember most is…; The reason… is that…; Looking back…*
- **Part 3** is more abstract: give an answer, a reason, an example, and if possible a contrasting point (*That said…, On the other hand…*).
