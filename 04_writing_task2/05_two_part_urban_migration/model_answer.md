# Model essay (Band 8–9)

Across much of the world, villages are emptying as people relocate to towns and cities. In this essay, I will explain the main reasons for this movement and argue that, despite some serious problems, it is broadly a positive development.

The principal driver of urban migration is economic opportunity. Cities concentrate industries, offices and services, and therefore offer a far wider range of jobs, usually at higher wages than those available in agriculture. As farming becomes more mechanised, fewer workers are needed in the countryside, pushing young people in particular to seek employment elsewhere. In addition, cities provide better access to education and healthcare. Families often move so that their children can attend good schools and universities, or so that they can be close to specialist hospitals that do not exist in rural areas.

In my opinion, this trend is largely beneficial. For individuals, moving to a city frequently brings higher incomes and greater choice in work, education and lifestyle. For countries, urbanisation tends to accompany economic growth, since businesses become more productive when workers and firms are clustered together. Providing services such as clean water, electricity and public transport is also more efficient when people live close together.

Nevertheless, the process creates genuine difficulties. Rapid migration can overwhelm urban infrastructure, resulting in overcrowded housing, informal settlements and traffic congestion, while rural communities may decline as young people leave and elderly residents remain. However, these problems are largely the result of poor planning rather than of urbanisation itself, and they can be mitigated by investment in affordable housing and by improving services in rural areas.

In conclusion, people are moving to cities mainly in search of work, education and healthcare. Although this brings challenges, I believe that its overall effects are positive, provided governments plan carefully for urban growth.
