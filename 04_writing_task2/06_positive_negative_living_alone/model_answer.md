# Model essay (Band 8–9)

Single-person households have become increasingly common in recent decades, particularly in wealthy, urbanised societies. While this trend reflects greater personal freedom, I would argue that its consequences for society as a whole are, on balance, more negative than positive.

There are undeniable benefits for the individuals concerned. Living alone allows people to organise their homes and daily routines exactly as they wish, without the compromises required when sharing space with others. For many young professionals, it represents a period of independence in which they can focus on their careers and personal development. For older people, especially those who have been widowed, remaining in their own home can preserve a sense of dignity and autonomy that might be lost in a care facility.

However, the wider effects of this trend are more troubling. Firstly, it places considerable pressure on the housing market. When one person occupies a dwelling that could accommodate a family, demand for homes rises and prices increase, making housing less affordable for everyone. It is also less environmentally efficient, since each household requires its own heating, appliances and often a car. Secondly, and perhaps most seriously, living alone is associated with a greater risk of loneliness, particularly among the elderly. Research in many countries has linked social isolation to depression and poorer physical health, which ultimately increases the burden on public health services.

In conclusion, although living alone can offer valuable independence, I believe it is a largely negative development because of its impact on housing affordability, the environment and public health. Governments and communities should therefore encourage alternatives such as shared housing and intergenerational living schemes that combine privacy with companionship.
