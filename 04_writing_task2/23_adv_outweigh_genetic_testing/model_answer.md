# Model essay (Band 8–9)

For a relatively small fee, people can now send a sample of saliva to a company and receive information about their ethnic origins and their genetic risk of developing certain diseases. Although these tests offer some genuine benefits, I believe their disadvantages, particularly regarding privacy and the misinterpretation of results, currently outweigh them.

There are clear attractions to home DNA testing. Many people find it fascinating to learn about their ancestry, and some have even discovered previously unknown relatives. More importantly, health reports can alert individuals to an increased risk of conditions such as certain cancers or heart disease, encouraging them to seek medical advice, attend screening or adopt a healthier lifestyle.

However, the drawbacks are serious. Genetic data is uniquely personal: unlike a password, it cannot be changed if it is stolen, and it reveals information not only about the customer but also about their relatives, who have not given consent. Some testing companies have shared data with third parties, and data breaches have exposed the genetic information of large numbers of customers. There is also a risk that such information could one day be used by insurers or employers to discriminate against people.

A further concern is that results are easily misunderstood. Most diseases are influenced by many genes as well as by lifestyle and environment, so a 'high-risk' result may cause unnecessary anxiety, while a 'low-risk' result may give false reassurance and discourage people from having medical checks. Without the guidance of a genetic counsellor, customers may draw the wrong conclusions.

In conclusion, although home DNA tests can satisfy curiosity and occasionally provide useful health warnings, the risks to privacy and the potential for misleading results mean that, at present, their disadvantages outweigh their advantages.
