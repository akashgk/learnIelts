# Model essay (Band 8–9)

Thanks to improvements in healthcare and falling birth rates, older people make up a growing share of the population in many countries. This demographic shift brings some notable benefits, but it also presents serious economic and social challenges.

On the positive side, longer lives mean that older people can continue to contribute to society for many more years. Many remain in work beyond the traditional retirement age, bringing valuable experience and skills to their organisations. Others play a vital role in their families and communities, for instance by caring for grandchildren, which allows parents to work, or by volunteering for charities. In addition, an older population tends to have lower crime rates, and the growing number of older consumers has created new markets in areas such as healthcare technology, leisure and tourism.

However, the disadvantages are significant. The most pressing is the financial burden on public finances. As the proportion of retired people rises and the working-age population shrinks, there are fewer taxpayers to fund state pensions and healthcare, whose costs increase sharply with age. Governments may therefore be forced to raise taxes, cut services or increase the retirement age, all of which are politically unpopular. A further problem is the shortage of care workers: many countries already struggle to find enough staff to look after elderly people who can no longer live independently, placing heavy pressure on family members.

In conclusion, an ageing population offers benefits in the form of experience, family support and new economic opportunities, but it also strains pension systems, healthcare and social care. Careful planning, such as encouraging flexible working for older people and investing in care services, will be needed to manage this transition successfully.
