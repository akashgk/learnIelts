# Model essay (Band 8–9)

In many countries, notes and coins are rapidly disappearing from everyday life as people pay by card, smartphone or smartwatch instead. In my view, this is largely a positive development, but only if governments ensure that vulnerable groups are not left behind.

The benefits of digital payments are considerable. For consumers, they are fast and convenient: there is no need to carry cash or find a cash machine, and spending can be tracked easily through banking apps, which may help people manage their budgets. For businesses, card payments reduce the time and cost of handling, counting and transporting cash, as well as the risk of theft. Digital transactions also leave a record, making it harder to evade taxes or launder money, which benefits society as a whole.

Nevertheless, a cashless society carries real risks. The most serious is exclusion. Elderly people, those on low incomes and people without bank accounts or smartphones may find it difficult to pay for essential goods if shops stop accepting cash. Moreover, digital systems are vulnerable to technical failures and cyber-attacks; a power cut or network outage could leave people unable to buy food. There are also concerns about privacy, as every electronic transaction can be recorded and potentially monitored.

On balance, I believe these problems can be managed. Governments can require essential services, such as supermarkets and pharmacies, to continue accepting cash, ensure that everyone has access to a basic bank account, and set strict rules on how payment data is used.

In conclusion, the decline of cash brings significant advantages in convenience, efficiency and transparency. Provided that measures are taken to protect vulnerable people and personal data, I consider it a positive development.
