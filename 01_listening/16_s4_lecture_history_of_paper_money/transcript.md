**LECTURER:** Today, in our economic history series, we're looking at the origins of paper money. It's something we take completely for granted, but the idea that a piece of paper with no value in itself can be exchanged for goods is actually quite remarkable.

**LECTURER:** Before paper money, most societies used coins made from precious metals, such as gold, silver or bronze. The problem was that large amounts of coins were extremely heavy. In China, where many coins were made of iron, this was a serious obstacle for merchants trading over long distances. **[Q31]**

**LECTURER:** The solution emerged in Sichuan province in the eleventh century. Merchants began leaving their heavy coins with trusted dealers in exchange for paper receipts, which could then be traded. Eventually, the government took over the issuing of these notes, creating the world's first official paper currency. **[Q32]**

**LECTURER:** When the Venetian traveller Marco Polo visited China in the thirteenth century, he was astonished that the emperor's subjects accepted paper made from the bark of mulberry trees as payment. **[Q33]** His descriptions were met with disbelief in Europe.

**LECTURER:** However, the Chinese experience also revealed paper money's greatest danger. Because it was cheap to produce, governments were tempted to print more and more of it to pay their expenses. The result was inflation: prices rose dramatically and people lost confidence in the notes. **[Q34]** By the fifteenth century, China had largely abandoned paper money.

**LECTURER:** In Europe, the first banknotes were issued in 1661 by a bank in Stockholm, Sweden. Interestingly, the reason was again the weight of coins: Sweden used copper coins, and some large denominations were enormous plates of copper weighing several kilograms. **[Q35]** But the bank issued too many notes and failed within a few years.

**LECTURER:** Over the following centuries, the solution to the problem of trust was to link paper money to gold. Under the gold standard, which most major economies had adopted by the late nineteenth century, people could in principle exchange their notes for a fixed quantity of gold at the central bank. **[Q36]** This limited how much money could be printed.

**LECTURER:** The gold standard, however, had a major disadvantage: it made it very difficult for governments to respond to economic crises, because they couldn't increase the money supply. **[Q37]** During the Great Depression of the 1930s, countries that left the gold standard earlier generally recovered faster.

**LECTURER:** The final link to gold was broken in 1971, when the United States stopped allowing other countries to exchange dollars for gold. Since then, almost all currencies have been what we call 'fiat' money — their value depends entirely on trust in the government and the central bank that issues them. **[Q38]**

**LECTURER:** To maintain this trust, most central banks today are independent of politicians, and they aim to keep inflation low and stable — typically around two per cent a year. **[Q39]**

**LECTURER:** And of course, the story continues. In many countries, cash is now used for only a small proportion of payments, and several central banks are experimenting with digital currencies. **[Q40]** Some economists worry this could threaten privacy, since every transaction could be recorded. We'll look at digital currencies in more detail next week.
