**LECTURER:** Today we're looking at habits — behaviours that we perform automatically, often without being aware of them. Researchers estimate that a substantial proportion of our everyday actions, perhaps around forty per cent, are habitual rather than the result of conscious decisions. **[Q31]**

**LECTURER:** Psychologists often describe habits in terms of a 'loop' with three parts. First there's a cue — something in the environment that triggers the behaviour. It might be a time of day, a place, or an emotion. Then there's the routine, the behaviour itself. And finally there's the reward, which makes the brain more likely to repeat the loop in future. **[Q32]**

**LECTURER:** Neuroscientists have found that as a behaviour becomes habitual, control shifts from the prefrontal cortex, which is involved in planning and decision-making, to deeper structures in the brain called the basal ganglia. **[Q33]** This is efficient — it frees up our attention for other things — but it also explains why habits are so hard to break: they no longer depend on conscious thought.

**LECTURER:** A popular myth claims that it takes twenty-one days to form a new habit. In fact, a well-known study at University College London followed volunteers who were trying to adopt a new daily behaviour, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or going for a run. On average, it took sixty-six days for the behaviour to become automatic, although the range was enormous — from under three weeks to over eight months. **[Q34]** Interestingly, missing a single day didn't significantly affect the process. **[Q35]** So the occasional lapse is nothing to worry about.

**LECTURER:** So how can we build good habits? One of the most effective techniques is called 'implementation intentions'. Rather than making a vague goal like 'I'll exercise more', people specify exactly when and where they'll act: 'After I get home from work, I'll put on my running shoes.' Linking a new behaviour to an existing habit in this way is sometimes called 'habit stacking'. **[Q36]**

**LECTURER:** Another strategy is to change the environment. If you want to eat more healthily, keeping fruit on the kitchen table rather than in a drawer makes it more visible and therefore more likely to be chosen. **[Q37]** Conversely, adding small obstacles — what researchers call 'friction' — can reduce unwanted behaviours. Putting your phone in another room while studying is a simple example.

**LECTURER:** Breaking bad habits is harder than forming good ones. Simply trying to suppress a habit through willpower tends to fail, partly because the cue remains. A more successful approach is to keep the same cue and reward but substitute a different routine. Someone who snacks when stressed, for example, might go for a short walk instead. **[Q38]**

**LECTURER:** Finally, there's evidence that major life changes create opportunities. When people move house or start a new job, their old cues disappear, and they're more open to new behaviours. One study found that people who had recently moved were more likely to start using public transport after being given information about it. **[Q39]** This has been called the 'habit discontinuity' hypothesis, and it has practical implications for governments and businesses, who may get the best results by targeting messages at people going through these transitions. **[Q40]**
