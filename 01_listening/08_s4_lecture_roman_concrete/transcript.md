# Transcript — Section 4: Lecture on Roman concrete

**LECTURER:** Good morning. In today's lecture in our series on materials science, I'm going to talk about one of the most durable building materials ever made: Roman concrete. Many Roman structures are still standing after two thousand years, while a lot of modern concrete starts to crack and crumble within fifty to a hundred years. So what did the Romans know that we've forgotten?

**LECTURER:** Let's start with the ingredients. Modern concrete is made with Portland cement, which was developed in the nineteenth century. The Romans used lime — made by heating limestone — mixed with a special volcanic ash. The best ash came from the area around the town of Pozzuoli, near Naples, which is why this kind of material is still called 'pozzolana' today. **[Q31]** They then added chunks of rock or broken brick, which formed the bulk of the material. **[Q32]**

**LECTURER:** The most famous example of what this material could achieve is the Pantheon in Rome, completed around 125 AD. Its dome, more than forty-three metres across, remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. **[Q33]** The Roman builders were clever about weight: towards the top of the dome they used lighter volcanic rock, called pumice, in the mix, so the dome becomes lighter as it rises. **[Q34]**

**LECTURER:** Even more remarkable are Roman harbour structures — piers and breakwaters built directly in the sea. Modern concrete exposed to seawater tends to deteriorate, because salt corrodes the steel bars inside it. Roman marine concrete, on the other hand, seems to have become stronger over time. Researchers analysing samples found that seawater seeping into the concrete reacted with the volcanic ash to form new minerals, including rare crystals that grow in the gaps and reinforce the material. **[Q35]** In other words, a process that destroys modern concrete actually strengthened the Roman version. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder had noticed something similar, writing that concrete in the sea became 'a single stone mass'.

**LECTURER:** A second secret was discovered more recently. Roman concrete contains small white lumps of lime, which scientists used to assume were simply evidence of poor mixing. But a study published in 2023 suggested that these lumps were the result of mixing the lime while it was still hot, in a form called quicklime. **[Q36]** And they play a crucial role. When a crack forms and water gets in, the water dissolves some of the lime from these lumps, which then recrystallises and fills the crack. The concrete, in effect, repairs itself. **[Q37]**

**LECTURER:** So why don't we simply go back to the Roman recipe? There are a few problems. First, Roman concrete is much weaker under compression when it's first made, and it takes a long time to reach full strength — which doesn't suit modern construction schedules. **[Q38]** Second, the Romans didn't use steel reinforcement, and modern buildings such as skyscrapers and long bridges depend on steel to cope with tension.

**LECTURER:** But there are good reasons to learn from the Romans. Producing Portland cement requires heating limestone to around 1,450 degrees Celsius, and the cement industry is responsible for roughly eight per cent of global carbon dioxide emissions. Roman lime was produced at significantly lower temperatures, so a Roman-inspired cement could reduce energy use. **[Q39]** And if concrete lasted centuries rather than decades, we would need to replace structures far less often. Researchers are now testing self-healing mixtures for use in sea walls, where durability matters more than speed. **[Q40]**
