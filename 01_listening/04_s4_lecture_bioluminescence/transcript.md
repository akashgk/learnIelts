# Transcript — Section 4: Lecture on bioluminescence

**LECTURER:** Good afternoon. Today's lecture is about bioluminescence — the production of light by living organisms. It's a phenomenon that has fascinated people for thousands of years; the Greek philosopher Aristotle described the 'cold light' given off by dead fish, and sailors have long reported seas that glow at night.

**LECTURER:** Let's start with the chemistry. In almost all cases, bioluminescence involves a reaction between two substances. The first is a molecule called luciferin, and the second is an enzyme called luciferase. When luciferin reacts with oxygen, with the help of luciferase, energy is released in the form of light. **[Q31]** What's remarkable is how efficient this process is. An ordinary old-fashioned light bulb loses most of its energy as heat, but in bioluminescence almost no heat is produced at all — which is why it's often called 'cold light'. **[Q32]**

**LECTURER:** Now, where is bioluminescence found? On land it's fairly rare. The best-known examples are fireflies, along with certain fungi and worms. But in the oceans it's extraordinarily common. A survey of the deep sea off the coast of California found that roughly three-quarters of the animals observed were capable of producing light. **[Q33]** Most marine bioluminescence is blue or green, and there's a good reason for that: these wavelengths travel furthest through seawater. **[Q34]**

**LECTURER:** So what is it used for? Biologists generally group the functions into three categories. The first is finding food. The anglerfish is the classic example: it has a glowing lure that hangs in front of its mouth, attracting smaller fish, which it then eats. **[Q35]**

**LECTURER:** The second function is defence. Some squid and shrimp, when attacked, release a cloud of glowing liquid — rather like the way other squid release ink — which confuses the predator while they escape. Another defensive strategy is called counter-illumination. Many animals in the middle depths produce light on their underside, which matches the faint light coming from the surface. Seen from below, this hides their shadow, so predators looking upwards can't see them. **[Q36]**

**LECTURER:** The third function is communication, and particularly attracting a partner. Fireflies are the obvious example: each species has its own pattern of flashes, so that males and females of the same species can recognise each other. **[Q37]**

**LECTURER:** Interestingly, not all bioluminescent animals make their own light. The Hawaiian bobtail squid, for example, has a special organ in which it keeps bacteria that produce light. The squid provides the bacteria with sugar and a safe place to live, and in return it gets light for counter-illumination. Every morning, the squid expels most of the bacteria into the water, and the population grows back during the day. **[Q38]**

**LECTURER:** Finally, let's look at some practical applications. In the 1960s, researchers studying a jellyfish discovered a protein that glows green under blue or ultraviolet light. This green fluorescent protein, or GFP, has since become one of the most important tools in biology, because scientists can attach it to other proteins and watch them move inside living cells. The work was considered so significant that it was awarded a Nobel Prize in 2008. **[Q39]**

**LECTURER:** Other applications are still being developed. Some researchers are working on bioluminescent trees that could replace street lights, although at present the light they produce is far too dim. More realistically, glowing bacteria are already used in tests for water pollution: when toxic chemicals are present, the bacteria produce less light, giving a quick and cheap warning. **[Q40]**
