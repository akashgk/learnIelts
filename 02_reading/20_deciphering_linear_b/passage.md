# Cracking an Ancient Code

In 1900, the British archaeologist Arthur Evans began excavating the palace of Knossos on the island of Crete. Among the treasures he uncovered were thousands of small clay tablets inscribed with an unknown script. The tablets had been preserved by accident: they were never meant to be permanent, but a fire that destroyed the palace baked them hard. Evans identified two related scripts, which he named Linear A and Linear B, and he devoted much of the rest of his life to studying them. Yet he made little progress in reading them, partly because he was convinced that the language they recorded was not Greek but belonged to an earlier, entirely separate Cretan civilisation, which he called Minoan.

Evans was also slow to publish the tablets in full, which meant that for decades other scholars had access to only a fraction of the material. Interest grew after 1939, when the American archaeologist Carl Blegen found hundreds more Linear B tablets at Pylos, on the Greek mainland. This was significant, since it showed the script was used beyond Crete, but Evans's authority was so great that most scholars continued to accept his view about the language.

The decisive groundwork was laid by Alice Kober, a classicist at Brooklyn College in New York. Working largely alone, in her spare time, and keeping her notes on thousands of handmade index cards, Kober took a rigorously methodical approach. Rather than guessing which language lay behind the script, she analysed the internal patterns of the writing itself. She noticed that certain words appeared in three slightly different forms, sharing a common beginning but with different endings — now known as 'Kober's triplets'. She concluded that the language must be inflected, changing the endings of words to show grammatical relationships, as Latin and Greek do. She also showed that certain signs must share the same consonant while differing in their vowels. Kober died in 1950, at the age of forty-three, before the script was deciphered.

The decipherment itself was achieved by Michael Ventris, an English architect who had been fascinated by the problem since hearing Evans lecture when he was a schoolboy. Building on Kober's work, Ventris arranged the signs in a grid, with signs believed to share a consonant in the same row and those sharing a vowel in the same column. He did not need to know the actual sounds to construct the grid; it simply recorded relationships. Like Evans, he initially assumed the language was not Greek, and he even suspected a link with Etruscan, an ancient language of Italy.

The breakthrough came in 1952. Ventris tried assigning sound values to a few signs, using words that appeared frequently on the tablets from Knossos and that he guessed might be place names on Crete, such as Amnisos and Knossos itself. Because the grid connected each sign to others, these few guesses produced values for many more signs. To his surprise, words began to emerge that looked like an early form of Greek. Ventris announced his findings in a radio talk and then sought the help of John Chadwick, a Cambridge expert on early Greek, who confirmed that the language was indeed Greek — about 500 years older than the works of Homer.

The decipherment was strongly confirmed in 1953, when Blegen applied Ventris's values to a newly discovered tablet from Pylos. It contained drawings of three-legged pots alongside a word that, read with the new values, spelled out the Greek word for 'tripod'. The tablets turned out to be mainly administrative records — lists of goods, livestock, workers and offerings — rather than literature or history. Linear A, meanwhile, remains undeciphered to this day.
