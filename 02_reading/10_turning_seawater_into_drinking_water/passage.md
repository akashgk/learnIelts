# Fresh Water from the Sea

**A**
Water covers more than 70 per cent of the Earth's surface, yet around 97 per cent of it is salt water that cannot be drunk or used for most kinds of farming. As populations grow and droughts become more frequent, an increasing number of countries are turning to desalination — the removal of salt from seawater — to secure their water supplies. There are now well over 15,000 desalination plants operating worldwide, concentrated particularly in the Middle East, North Africa, Australia and parts of the United States.

**B**
The oldest method is thermal desalination, which imitates the natural water cycle. Seawater is heated until it evaporates, leaving the salt behind, and the vapour is then cooled so that it condenses into fresh water. The process is reliable but consumes enormous quantities of energy, which is why it has been most popular in oil-rich Gulf states, where fuel has historically been cheap. Since the 1970s, however, it has gradually been overtaken by a different technology: reverse osmosis.

**C**
In reverse osmosis, seawater is forced at very high pressure through a membrane containing pores so tiny that water molecules can pass through but salt particles cannot. Before reaching the membranes, the water must be carefully filtered to remove sand, algae and other material that would otherwise block them. Improvements in membrane design and in devices that recover energy from the high-pressure waste stream have reduced the electricity needed to produce a cubic metre of fresh water by more than half since the 1990s. Today, reverse osmosis accounts for the large majority of new desalination capacity.

**D**
Despite these advances, desalinated water remains considerably more expensive than water taken from rivers or underground sources, and the plants are costly to build. Their energy use also raises environmental questions. If the electricity comes from fossil fuels, desalination contributes to the very climate change that is making droughts worse — a vicious circle that critics are quick to point out. For this reason, several new plants have been designed to run on solar or wind power.

**E**
A second environmental concern is brine, the concentrated salt water left over after fresh water has been extracted. For every litre of drinking water, a typical plant produces roughly one and a half litres of brine, which is usually pumped back into the sea. Because brine is denser than seawater, it can sink and spread along the seabed, reducing oxygen levels and harming organisms that live there. Engineers try to reduce the damage by releasing brine through pipes fitted with many outlets, so that it mixes quickly with the surrounding water. Researchers are also investigating ways of extracting useful minerals such as magnesium and lithium from brine, which could turn a waste product into a source of income.

**F**
In my view, desalination should be regarded as a last resort rather than a first choice. Many water-stressed cities lose a substantial share of their supply through leaking pipes, and recycling wastewater or encouraging households to use less water is usually far cheaper than building a desalination plant. Nonetheless, for coastal regions with few alternatives, it can provide a valuable insurance policy against drought, particularly if it is powered by renewable energy.
