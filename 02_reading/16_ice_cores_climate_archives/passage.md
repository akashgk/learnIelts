# Reading the Ice

In the interior of Antarctica and Greenland, snow has been falling and accumulating for hundreds of thousands of years. Because temperatures rarely rise above freezing, it never melts. Instead, each year's snowfall is buried by the next, and the growing weight gradually compresses the lower layers into dense ice. Over time, these ice sheets have become a frozen archive of the Earth's past climate — and since the 1950s, scientists have been drilling into them to read it.

The process of retrieving an ice core is slow and technically demanding. Drilling teams use a hollow drill that cuts a cylinder of ice around ten centimetres in diameter, bringing it to the surface in sections of a few metres at a time. The deepest cores, which may reach more than three kilometres below the surface, can take several summer seasons to complete. Once extracted, the sections are cut, labelled, packed in insulated boxes and shipped to laboratories, where they are stored at temperatures of around minus 30°C to prevent any changes to their chemistry.

Scientists can extract several kinds of information from the ice. The first is temperature. Water molecules contain different forms, or isotopes, of oxygen and hydrogen, and the proportion of heavier isotopes that falls as snow depends on the air temperature at the time. By measuring these proportions layer by layer, researchers can estimate how temperatures changed over time. The second is the composition of the atmosphere itself. As snow turns into ice, tiny bubbles of air become trapped inside it. These bubbles are samples of ancient atmosphere, allowing scientists to measure directly how much carbon dioxide and methane were present thousands of years ago.

Ice cores also record dramatic events. Large volcanic eruptions release sulphur into the atmosphere, which then falls with snow and leaves a distinct chemical signature in the ice. Layers of dust indicate periods when the climate was drier and windier. Because the upper layers can be counted year by year, like the rings of a tree, these signals can be dated with considerable precision; deeper down, where layers become too thin to distinguish, dating relies on computer models of how ice flows and on matching the record with other sources of evidence.

The most famous result of ice-core research comes from a core drilled at a site in East Antarctica known as Dome C, which covered roughly 800,000 years. It revealed that the Earth's climate had alternated between long, cold glacial periods and shorter, warmer interglacial periods about eight times. Crucially, temperature and carbon dioxide levels rose and fell closely together throughout this time. During the warm periods, carbon dioxide concentrations reached about 280 to 300 parts per million; during ice ages, they fell to around 180. Today's level, above 420 parts per million, is far higher than at any point in the entire record.

Researchers are now searching for even older ice. An international project is drilling at a site in Antarctica where models suggest the ice at the bottom may be 1.5 million years old. Such a record could help to explain a puzzling change that occurred around a million years ago, when the cycles between ice ages became longer and more intense for reasons that are still not understood.
