# The Bilingual Brain

For much of the twentieth century, raising a child with two languages was widely considered a risk. Early studies, many conducted in the 1920s and 1930s, reported that bilingual children scored lower on intelligence tests than their monolingual peers, and parents were sometimes advised to speak only the dominant language of the country at home. With hindsight, these studies were deeply flawed. They frequently compared children from poor immigrant families with wealthier native speakers, and tested the bilingual children in their weaker language. When researchers in the 1960s began to control for social background, the supposed disadvantage disappeared.

The pendulum then swung in the opposite direction. From the late 1990s onwards, a series of influential experiments suggested that bilingualism might actually confer cognitive benefits. The reasoning was intuitively appealing. A person who speaks two languages has both of them active in the mind at all times, even when using only one, and must constantly suppress the language that is not needed. This continuous mental juggling, researchers proposed, might strengthen 'executive function' — the set of abilities that allows us to focus attention, ignore distractions and switch between tasks. In laboratory tasks requiring participants to respond to one feature of a stimulus while ignoring another, bilingual children and adults often responded faster than monolinguals.

Even more strikingly, some studies of hospital patients reported that bilinguals developed the symptoms of dementia several years later than monolinguals with similar levels of brain damage. This finding was interpreted as evidence of 'cognitive reserve': the idea that a lifetime of mental exercise allows the brain to cope with damage for longer before function declines. Newspapers enthusiastically reported that learning a language could protect against Alzheimer's disease.

In the past decade, however, the 'bilingual advantage' has come under sustained criticism. Several large studies, some involving thousands of participants, have failed to find any difference in executive function between bilinguals and monolinguals. Critics have also pointed to a problem known as publication bias: experiments that find an interesting effect are more likely to be submitted to and accepted by scientific journals than those that find nothing, so the published literature may give an exaggerated impression of the effect. One analysis of conference presentations found that studies supporting a bilingual advantage were far more likely to end up in print than those that did not. Moreover, the dementia findings, which came largely from patients attending particular clinics, have not been consistently confirmed in studies that follow whole populations over time.

Defenders of the bilingual advantage respond that 'bilingualism' is not a single experience. Someone who learned two languages from birth and switches between them many times a day may be affected very differently from someone who studied a second language at school and rarely uses it. Treating all these people as a single group, they argue, could easily hide real effects. This is a fair point, although it also makes the original claim much harder to test, since almost any negative result can then be explained away.

What, then, should parents and educators conclude? In my view, the debate over executive function, though scientifically interesting, is largely beside the point. Even if bilingualism provides no measurable boost to attention, there is no credible evidence that it causes harm, and it brings obvious benefits: the ability to communicate with more people, access to other cultures and, often, closer ties with family members. These are reasons enough to support children in learning and keeping more than one language.
