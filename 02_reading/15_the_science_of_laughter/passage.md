# Why We Laugh

**A**
Most people assume that laughter is mainly a response to jokes. Yet when the American psychologist Robert Provine and his students secretly recorded more than a thousand episodes of laughter in shopping centres, university campuses and other public places, they found that only a small minority — around 10 to 20 per cent — followed anything resembling a joke. Far more often, people laughed after ordinary remarks such as 'I'll see you later' or 'Are you sure?'. Provine concluded that laughter is primarily a social signal rather than a reaction to humour.

**B**
Supporting this view is the finding that people are far more likely to laugh when they are with others. In one study, participants were around thirty times more likely to laugh in company than when alone. Speakers also tend to laugh more than their listeners, which suggests that laughter is used to show friendliness and to keep a conversation relaxed. Interestingly, laughter rarely interrupts the flow of speech; it almost always occurs at pauses, at the end of phrases or sentences, in the same way as punctuation in writing.

**C**
Laughter is far older than language. Chimpanzees, gorillas and other great apes produce a breathy, panting sound during play, especially when being chased or tickled, and many researchers believe this 'play face' and panting sound are the evolutionary origins of human laughter. Even rats appear to produce high-pitched chirping sounds when tickled by researchers — sounds beyond the range of human hearing — and seek out the hand that tickles them. This suggests that the basic brain circuits for playful vocalisation may be shared by many mammals.

**D**
One of the most striking features of laughter is that it is contagious. Hearing someone else laugh activates areas of the brain involved in preparing facial movements, making us ready to join in. This is why television comedies have traditionally used recorded laughter: studies show that audiences laugh more, and rate jokes as funnier, when they can hear others laughing. In 1962, an extreme case was reported at a girls' school in what is now Tanzania, where an outbreak of uncontrollable laughter spread to hundreds of people in nearby villages and caused several schools to close temporarily.

**E**
Laughter may also be good for us. Laughing with others has been shown to increase people's tolerance of pain, possibly because it triggers the release of endorphins, the body's natural painkillers. In one experiment, people who had watched a comedy video together were able to hold their arms in uncomfortable positions for longer than those who had watched a documentary. Laughter also appears to strengthen social bonds: people who laugh together report feeling closer and are more willing to share personal information.

**F**
Not all laughter is the same, however. Researchers distinguish between spontaneous laughter, which is involuntary and genuinely felt, and voluntary or 'social' laughter, which people produce deliberately to be polite. Listeners can usually tell the difference: in experiments, people from a wide range of cultures were able to identify whether recordings of laughter were spontaneous or deliberate at rates well above chance. Spontaneous laughter tends to be higher in pitch and contains sounds that would be difficult to produce on purpose.
