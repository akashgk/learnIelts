# The Invisible Pollutant

**A**
When people imagine plastic pollution, they usually picture bottles washed up on beaches or turtles trapped in fishing nets. Yet some of the most widespread plastic pollution cannot be seen with the naked eye. Microplastics — pieces of plastic smaller than five millimetres — have now been found almost everywhere scientists have looked: in deep-sea sediments, Arctic sea ice, mountain air, drinking water, table salt and human blood.

**B**
Microplastics come from two main sources. 'Primary' microplastics are manufactured at a small size, such as the tiny beads once commonly added to face scrubs and toothpaste, many of which have now been banned in a number of countries. Far more abundant, however, are 'secondary' microplastics, which form when larger items break down under the effects of sunlight, waves and friction. Surprisingly large quantities come from everyday activities: synthetic clothing releases thousands of fibres in a single wash, and car tyres shed particles as they wear against road surfaces.

**C**
Once in the environment, microplastics travel remarkable distances. Rivers carry them to the sea, ocean currents distribute them across the globe, and the smallest particles can be lifted into the atmosphere and fall back to earth in rain or snow. Studies of remote mountain areas in Europe have recorded microplastics in places far from any town or road, suggesting that they had been carried there by the wind.

**D**
A wide range of animals, from tiny zooplankton to whales, swallow microplastics, either by mistake or because the particles are covered with algae that make them smell like food. Laboratory experiments have shown that high concentrations can reduce feeding and growth in some marine species. Microplastics may also act as carriers for other pollutants, since harmful chemicals in seawater tend to stick to their surfaces. However, many laboratory studies have used concentrations far higher than those typically found in the sea, so it is not yet clear how much harm occurs in natural conditions.

**E**
The question that concerns the public most is the effect on human health. People are thought to take in microplastics mainly by eating and drinking, and by breathing in particles from indoor air, where fibres from carpets and clothing accumulate. Some particles have been detected in human organs, but researchers emphasise that detecting them is not the same as demonstrating harm, and that measuring such tiny particles accurately is technically very difficult. Contamination from plastic laboratory equipment, or even from researchers' own clothing, can distort results.

**F**
Because microplastics are so difficult to remove once they have entered the environment, most experts argue that the priority should be to stop them being released. Proposed measures include filters for washing machines — which France has required to be fitted to new models from 2025 — improved road drainage systems to capture tyre particles, and, above all, reducing the overall production of single-use plastics.
