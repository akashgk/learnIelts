# Finding the Way Home

Every autumn, billions of birds leave their breeding grounds in the northern hemisphere and fly south, some travelling more than 10,000 kilometres before returning the following spring, often to the same nest. Young sea turtles that hatch on a beach in Florida swim into the Atlantic, circle the ocean for years and, as adults, return to nest on the coast where they were born. How animals accomplish such feats of navigation has puzzled naturalists for centuries, and although much remains unknown, research over the past fifty years has revealed a remarkable range of strategies.

Many animals use the sun as a compass. Because the sun's position changes throughout the day, this requires an internal clock that allows the animal to compensate for its movement across the sky. In a famous series of experiments in the 1950s, the German ornithologist Gustav Kramer showed that caged starlings oriented themselves in the correct migratory direction when they could see the sun, but became disoriented under overcast skies. When he used mirrors to shift the apparent position of the sun, the birds changed their orientation by a corresponding angle. Night-flying birds, by contrast, rely partly on the stars. Experiments conducted in planetariums by the American researcher Stephen Emlen in the 1960s demonstrated that young indigo buntings learn to identify the point around which the night sky appears to rotate — in the northern hemisphere, near the Pole Star — and use it to determine the direction of north.

A compass alone, however, is not enough for true navigation. To find a specific destination from an unfamiliar place, an animal needs something equivalent to a map: a way of knowing where it is in relation to its goal. Here the Earth's magnetic field appears to play a crucial role. The field varies in both strength and angle across the planet's surface, so that each location has, in effect, a distinctive magnetic 'signature'. The marine biologist Kenneth Lohmann and his colleagues exposed hatchling loggerhead turtles to magnetic fields replicating those found at different points around the North Atlantic. The turtles, swimming in a tank, consistently turned in directions that would keep them within the warm currents of their migratory route, suggesting that they possess an inherited map based on magnetism.

How animals detect magnetic fields is one of the most intriguing unsolved problems in biology. One hypothesis proposes that birds have tiny particles of a magnetic iron mineral in their bodies that act like compass needles. Another, more recent theory suggests that the detection takes place in the eye, through a chemical reaction in a light-sensitive protein called cryptochrome, which may be affected by magnetic fields. If correct, this would mean that birds can, in some sense, 'see' the magnetic field. The two mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and some researchers believe that birds may use both for different purposes.

Smell may also contribute to navigation, at least for some species. Homing pigeons whose sense of smell has been temporarily blocked have been found to have greater difficulty returning home from unfamiliar release sites, and seabirds such as shearwaters appear to use odours carried by the wind to locate their colonies across vast stretches of open ocean.

Navigation is not only a matter of senses but also of learning. Young birds on their first migration typically follow an inherited direction and distance, but if they are blown off course, they often fail to correct for it. Experienced adults, by contrast, can compensate, which suggests that they have developed a more sophisticated map through experience. Understanding these mechanisms is not merely of academic interest: artificial lighting, electromagnetic noise from human activity and changes in climate may all interfere with the cues on which migrating animals depend.
