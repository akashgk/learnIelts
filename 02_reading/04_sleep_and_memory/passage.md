# Why We Sleep on It

**A**
For much of the twentieth century, sleep was regarded as a passive state — a period during which the brain simply switched off to rest. Today, neuroscientists view it very differently. A growing body of evidence suggests that sleep plays an active and essential role in learning, and that some of the most important work involved in forming long-term memories takes place not while we study, but while we are unconscious.

**B**
Sleep is not uniform. Throughout the night, the brain cycles through several stages roughly every 90 minutes. These include light sleep, deep or 'slow-wave' sleep, during which electrical activity in the brain forms large, slow waves, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the stage most closely associated with vivid dreaming. Slow-wave sleep dominates the first half of the night, while REM periods become longer towards the morning. This pattern has practical consequences: a person who goes to bed late and wakes early loses a disproportionate amount of REM sleep, whereas someone who sleeps for only the first few hours loses less slow-wave sleep.

**C**
Different stages appear to support different kinds of memory. Experiments in which volunteers learn lists of word pairs in the evening and are tested the following morning consistently show that performance is closely linked to the amount of slow-wave sleep they obtained. This kind of memory — for facts and events — depends heavily on a small, curved structure deep in the brain called the hippocampus. According to one widely accepted theory, the hippocampus acts as a temporary store; during slow-wave sleep, recently acquired information is 'replayed' and gradually transferred to the outer layer of the brain, the cortex, where it can be kept for years. REM sleep, by contrast, seems more important for procedural skills such as playing a musical instrument, and for the emotional processing of experiences.

**D**
Some of the most striking evidence comes from studies of rats. When a rat runs through a maze, particular cells in its hippocampus fire in a specific sequence that corresponds to its location. Researchers recording from these cells found that the same sequence was repeated while the animal slept afterwards, sometimes at many times the original speed. Remarkably, when scientists interrupted this replay with small electrical pulses, the rats were later slower to learn the route, suggesting that replay is not merely a by-product of learning but a necessary part of it.

**E**
These findings have encouraged attempts to improve memory by manipulating sleep directly. In one technique, known as targeted memory reactivation, participants learn information while a particular sound or smell is present. When the same cue is presented again during slow-wave sleep — at a level too low to wake them — their recall of the associated material tends to improve. Other researchers have used gentle sound pulses timed to coincide with slow brain waves, reporting modest increases in both the depth of sleep and next-day memory. Such methods remain experimental, however, and it is not yet clear whether they would bring meaningful benefits outside the laboratory.

**F**
For students, the implications are nevertheless clear. Staying up all night before an examination may allow more hours of study, but it deprives the brain of the very process that would help fix the material in memory. Studies of 'all-nighters' have found that a single night without sleep can reduce the ability to form new memories by as much as 40 per cent. Spreading study over several days, with a full night's sleep in between, appears to be a far more effective strategy. As one researcher has observed, sleep is not the enemy of productivity but one of its most powerful tools.
