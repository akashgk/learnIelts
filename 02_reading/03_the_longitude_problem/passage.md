# The Longitude Problem

For most of history, sailors crossing the open ocean faced a dangerous uncertainty. Measuring latitude — a ship's position north or south of the equator — was relatively straightforward: a navigator could calculate it from the height of the sun at midday or the position of the Pole Star at night. Longitude, the position east or west, was a different matter entirely. Because the Earth rotates through 360 degrees every 24 hours, each hour of difference between local time and the time at a fixed reference point corresponds to 15 degrees of longitude. In theory, therefore, a navigator who knew both the local time on board and the time at the home port at that same moment could work out how far east or west the ship had travelled. In practice, no clock in the seventeenth century could keep accurate time on a rolling, pitching ship, where changes in temperature and humidity caused metal parts to expand and lubricating oils to thicken.

The consequences of this ignorance were frequently fatal. Ships ran aground on unexpected coastlines or sailed for weeks in the wrong direction while their crews died of scurvy. In 1707, a British naval fleet returning from the Mediterranean misjudged its position and struck the rocks of the Scilly Isles, off the south-west coast of England; four ships sank and well over a thousand sailors drowned. Partly in response to such disasters, the British Parliament passed the Longitude Act in 1714, offering a prize of £20,000 — a fortune at the time — to anyone who could devise a method of finding longitude at sea to within half a degree. A committee, later known as the Board of Longitude, was appointed to judge the entries.

Most scientists of the day believed the solution would be found in the sky. The 'lunar distance' method relied on measuring the angle between the Moon and a particular star, then comparing it with tables predicting where the Moon would be at given times as seen from a reference point. The method was sound in principle, but the Moon's movements are extremely complex, and producing accurate tables required decades of observation and laborious calculation. Even once the tables existed, a navigator needed up to four hours to complete the arithmetic for a single reading.

The person who eventually solved the problem by a different route had no formal education in astronomy at all. John Harrison was a carpenter from Lincolnshire who taught himself clockmaking, building his first clocks almost entirely from wood. Over a period of more than thirty years, he constructed a series of increasingly sophisticated marine timekeepers, introducing innovations such as a strip made from two different metals that compensated for changes in temperature. His first three machines were large and heavy, but his fourth, completed in 1759, resembled an oversized pocket watch and measured only about 13 centimetres across. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62, it lost just five seconds over more than two months at sea, comfortably meeting the requirements of the Act.

Harrison's reward, however, was slow to arrive. The Board of Longitude, dominated by astronomers, was suspicious of a solution that came from a craftsman rather than a scientist and doubted whether a single successful watch proved that the method could be reproduced. One of its members, the astronomer Nevil Maskelyne, was himself a strong supporter of the lunar method and published a nautical almanac that made lunar calculations considerably quicker. The Board demanded further trials and insisted that Harrison hand over his machines and explain their construction in detail. It was only after the intervention of King George III, who personally tested one of Harrison's watches, that Parliament awarded him a further substantial sum in 1773, three years before his death. He never officially received the prize itself.

In the long run, both approaches proved valuable. Marine chronometers based on Harrison's principles were expensive at first, so for several decades many ships continued to rely on lunar distances, using Maskelyne's almanac. As manufacturing techniques improved and prices fell, however, the chronometer became standard equipment on ocean-going vessels by the middle of the nineteenth century, and it remained so until radio time signals and, much later, satellite navigation made it obsolete.
