# The Penny Universities

The first coffee house in England is generally believed to have opened in Oxford in 1650, and London's first followed two years later, in an alley near the Royal Exchange. Within a few decades, the drink had become extraordinarily fashionable: by the early eighteenth century, contemporary estimates suggested that London had several hundred coffee houses, though the precise number is uncertain. Their popularity owed less to the taste of the coffee itself, which visitors often described as bitter and unpleasant, than to the atmosphere they offered.

Coffee houses were nicknamed 'penny universities' because, for the price of a penny admission, a customer could sit for hours, read the newspapers and pamphlets provided, and join conversations with strangers on almost any subject. In contrast to taverns, where alcohol was served, coffee houses encouraged sober discussion. Social rank mattered less than in most other public places; according to the rules displayed in some establishments, anyone was free to take any seat, and no one was obliged to give up their place to a person of higher status. Women, however, were generally not welcome as customers, although some coffee houses were run by women.

Different coffee houses attracted different groups. Merchants and shipowners gathered at those near the river and the Royal Exchange to exchange information about cargoes and prices. One of these, opened by Edward Lloyd around 1688, became known as a place where ship-owners could find people willing to insure their vessels. Lloyd himself was not an insurer, but he encouraged this business by collecting and publishing shipping news. Over time, the insurers who met there formed an organisation that still bears his name. Similarly, dealers in company shares, who had been expelled from the Royal Exchange for their rowdy behaviour, conducted their business in Jonathan's Coffee House, an arrangement that eventually led to the founding of the London Stock Exchange.

Scientists and intellectuals had their favourite haunts as well. Members of the Royal Society, the scientific association founded in 1660, often continued their discussions in coffee houses after their formal meetings. There are accounts of the astronomer Edmond Halley and other scholars debating scientific questions over coffee; on one occasion, Halley is said to have dissected a dolphin in front of fellow customers.

Not everyone approved. In 1675, King Charles II issued a proclamation ordering the closure of all coffee houses, which he regarded as places where false rumours and criticism of the government were spread. The order provoked such widespread protest from both customers and the coffee merchants who paid taxes on their trade that it was withdrawn within days, before it had even come into effect. Some critics complained of a different kind: a pamphlet published in 1674, supposedly written by women, claimed that men wasted so much time in coffee houses that they were neglecting their families.

By the late eighteenth century, the coffee house was in decline. Many developed into private clubs with paying members, while others became ordinary taverns or restaurants. At the same time, tea, promoted by the powerful East India Company, was steadily replacing coffee as the nation's favourite hot drink. Yet the coffee houses left a lasting legacy in the institutions that grew out of them and in the idea that public conversation between strangers could be a source of commercial and intellectual progress.
