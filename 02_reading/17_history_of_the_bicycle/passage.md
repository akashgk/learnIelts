# Two Wheels

**A**
The first machine that recognisably resembled a bicycle was invented in 1817 by Karl von Drais, a German civil servant. His 'running machine' had two wheels in line, a wooden frame and a steerable front wheel, but no pedals: riders pushed themselves along with their feet. According to one popular theory, Drais was looking for an alternative to the horse after a volcanic eruption in Indonesia two years earlier had caused crop failures across Europe, making horse feed scarce and expensive. Whatever his motivation, the machine enjoyed a brief period of fashion before interest faded, partly because riders were banned from pavements in several cities.

**B**
Pedals arrived in the 1860s, when French makers attached cranks directly to the front wheel. These machines, nicknamed 'boneshakers' because of their iron-rimmed wooden wheels and the rough roads of the time, were uncomfortable but popular. Since each turn of the pedals rotated the front wheel only once, the only way to travel faster was to make that wheel larger. The result was the 'high-wheeler', or penny-farthing, of the 1870s, whose front wheel could be more than a metre and a half in diameter. It was fast and, on good roads, surprisingly smooth, but it was also dangerous: riders sat high above the ground and were frequently thrown forward over the handlebars.

**C**
The breakthrough came in 1885, when the English inventor John Kemp Starley produced the 'Rover', the first commercially successful 'safety bicycle'. It had two wheels of similar size and a chain that transmitted the rider's pedalling to the rear wheel. Because the chain allowed gearing, a small wheel could travel as fast as a large one. Three years later, the Scottish inventor John Boyd Dunlop developed a practical air-filled tyre — originally for his son's tricycle — which greatly improved comfort. With these innovations, the basic design of the bicycle was essentially complete; a modern bicycle would be easily recognisable to a rider of the 1890s.

**D**
The result was a cycling boom. In the 1890s, mass production brought prices down, and millions of people bought bicycles in Europe and North America. For the first time, working people could travel independently beyond walking distance, which widened their choices of where to live, work and even whom to marry. Some historians have suggested that the bicycle contributed to greater genetic diversity in rural areas, as young people could more easily meet partners from neighbouring villages.

**E**
The bicycle also had a significant impact on women's lives. Cycling required more practical clothing than the long skirts and tight corsets of the time, and the 'rational dress' movement promoted divided skirts and loose trousers. Many campaigners for women's rights saw the bicycle as a symbol of independence. In 1896, the American campaigner Susan B. Anthony famously declared that bicycling had 'done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world'. Not everyone approved: some doctors warned that cycling could harm women's health, claims that now seem absurd.

**F**
The bicycle's influence extended to other technologies. Many techniques developed for bicycle manufacture, such as ball bearings, lightweight steel tubing and precision assembly, were later used to build cars and aircraft. The Wright brothers, who achieved the first powered flight in 1903, ran a bicycle shop in Ohio, and it was the profits from this business that funded their experiments.
