# Borrowed, Not Bought

In the late 2000s, a new kind of business began to attract attention. Rather than selling products or services directly, companies built online platforms that connected people who owned underused assets — a spare room, a car, a set of tools — with others who were willing to pay to use them for a short time. Enthusiasts described this as the 'sharing economy' and predicted that it would transform not only markets but also the way people related to possessions and to one another.

The economic logic was attractive. The average private car, for instance, is parked for well over 90 per cent of its life, and a power drill bought for home use may be operated for only a few minutes in total. If such items could be shared, owners would earn extra income while users would avoid the cost of buying something they rarely needed. Advocates also emphasised the environmental benefits: if fewer goods were manufactured, fewer resources would be consumed. Some went further, arguing that the sharing economy would rebuild a sense of community that had been weakened by urban anonymity.

Smartphones, GPS and online payment systems made this model practical on a large scale for the first time. Crucially, platforms also developed rating systems in which users and providers reviewed each other after every transaction. These ratings served as a substitute for the personal trust that had traditionally limited sharing to friends and neighbours, allowing strangers to stay in each other's homes or ride in each other's cars with reasonable confidence.

Within a decade, however, the picture had become considerably more complicated. The most successful platforms grew into some of the world's most valuable companies, and critics argued that much of what they offered had little to do with sharing. Many properties listed on home-rental platforms were not spare rooms in occupied homes but entire apartments bought specifically to be let to tourists. In popular cities such as Barcelona and Amsterdam, residents complained that this reduced the supply of long-term housing and pushed up rents, and local authorities introduced limits on the number of nights a property could be rented out each year.

Similar concerns were raised about ride-hailing services. Drivers were generally classified as independent contractors rather than employees, which meant they did not receive guaranteed minimum wages, sick pay or holiday pay. The companies argued that this arrangement offered drivers valuable flexibility, allowing them to work whenever they chose. Courts in several countries, however, have since ruled that some drivers should be treated as workers entitled to basic employment rights — decisions that the companies have strongly contested.

The environmental case has also been questioned. Some studies of ride-hailing in American cities found that it increased overall traffic, because many trips replaced journeys that would otherwise have been made by public transport, on foot or by bicycle, and because drivers spent considerable time driving without passengers between bookings.

In my view, it would be a mistake to dismiss the sharing economy altogether. Platforms for lending tools, swapping clothes or sharing meals among neighbours do encourage more efficient use of resources and genuine contact between people. The lesson is rather that the label 'sharing' was often applied too generously to businesses whose main purpose was profit. Judging each platform on what it actually does, rather than on how it describes itself, would allow societies to keep its benefits while limiting its costs.
