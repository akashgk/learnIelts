# Too Much of a Good Thing?

It seems obvious that having more options is better than having fewer. A shopper who can choose from fifty brands of breakfast cereal is surely more likely to find one that suits them than a shopper offered five. Classical economics has generally assumed exactly this: since a rational consumer can always ignore options they do not want, adding choices can never make anyone worse off. Over the past three decades, however, psychologists have challenged this assumption, arguing that beyond a certain point, choice can become a burden rather than a benefit.

The most widely cited evidence comes from an experiment conducted in a Californian supermarket around the turn of the millennium. On some days, researchers set up a tasting stand offering 24 varieties of jam; on others, the same stand displayed only six. The larger display attracted more passers-by to stop and taste. Yet when it came to buying, the pattern was reversed: around 30 per cent of shoppers who had seen the small selection went on to purchase a jar, compared with only about 3 per cent of those who had seen the large one. The researchers concluded that an extensive array of options could reduce people's motivation to choose at all — a phenomenon that came to be known as 'choice overload'.

The finding captured the public imagination. It was featured in best-selling books, cited in business schools and used to justify decisions ranging from simplifying product ranges to reducing the number of investment funds offered in pension schemes. The psychologist Barry Schwartz popularised the broader idea of a 'paradox of choice', suggesting that an abundance of options in modern life contributes not only to indecision but also to anxiety and regret. When there are many alternatives, he argued, it is easy to imagine that one of the rejected options would have been better, which reduces satisfaction with whatever is eventually chosen.

Schwartz also drew an influential distinction between two kinds of decision-maker. 'Maximisers' feel they must examine every possibility in order to find the very best option, while 'satisficers' — a term borrowed from the economist Herbert Simon — are content to select the first option that meets their requirements. Surveys suggest that maximisers often make objectively better choices, for example obtaining higher starting salaries when looking for jobs, yet report feeling less satisfied with the outcome than satisficers do.

Subsequent research, however, has painted a more complicated picture. When other teams attempted to repeat the jam study and similar experiments, the results were inconsistent: some found evidence of choice overload, some found none, and some even found that larger assortments increased purchasing. A statistical review that combined the results of dozens of studies concluded that the average effect of choice overload across all of them was close to zero. This does not necessarily mean the original finding was wrong. Rather, it suggests that choice overload is real only under certain conditions.

Researchers have since tried to identify what those conditions are. Overload appears more likely when the options are difficult to compare, when the decision-maker has no clear preferences in advance, when the choice must be made quickly, and when the options are not organised in any helpful way. A wine enthusiast confronted with a hundred bottles may enjoy the variety; a novice may find the same shelf paralysing. Similarly, grouping options into categories, or offering a sensible default, can make large selections much easier to manage.

The practical lesson, then, is not that choice is bad, nor that it is always good. It is that the effect of choice depends on how it is presented and on who is choosing. For designers of everything from restaurant menus to government services, the question is not simply 'how many options?' but 'how can people be helped to navigate them?'
