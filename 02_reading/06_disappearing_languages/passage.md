# When a Language Dies

Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today, linguists estimate that around 40 per cent are endangered, and that a substantial proportion may cease to be spoken by the end of this century. The distribution of speakers is strikingly uneven: fewer than a hundred languages account for the first languages of the great majority of the world's population, while thousands of others are spoken by communities of a few thousand people or fewer. Many of these smaller languages are not being learned by children, which linguists regard as the clearest sign that a language is in danger. A language that has adult speakers but no young ones is, in effect, on a countdown.

Languages have always died, of course. Latin, Sumerian and countless unrecorded tongues have disappeared over the centuries, usually as their speakers were absorbed into larger populations or gradually shifted to another language for economic or political reasons. What is unprecedented, many linguists argue, is the current rate of loss. Urbanisation draws young people away from rural communities where minority languages are spoken; national education systems typically teach in one or two dominant languages; and the global reach of television, the internet and commerce gives enormous advantages to a handful of world languages. In such conditions, parents may decide — often for entirely understandable reasons — that their children's prospects will be better if they are raised in the majority language.

Why should this matter? One argument is scientific. Each language represents a distinct solution to the problem of human communication, and the full range of possible grammatical structures can only be understood by studying as many languages as possible. Some languages, for example, have no words for 'left' and 'right', describing all locations instead in terms of compass directions; others mark in their grammar whether a speaker witnessed an event personally or heard about it from someone else. Each time a language disappears without being documented, the evidence it contains about the capacities of the human mind is lost for ever.

A second argument concerns knowledge. Communities that have lived in one environment for many generations often possess detailed understanding of local plants, animals, weather and medicine, and this knowledge is encoded in their vocabulary and oral traditions. When the language goes, much of this knowledge goes with it, because it is rarely translated in full. A third argument, and for many speakers the most important, concerns identity. For communities that have experienced colonisation or discrimination, the loss of a language is frequently felt as the loss of a connection with ancestors and a sense of who they are.

Not everyone accepts these arguments without reservation. Some economists have pointed out that a shared language reduces the costs of trade and communication, and that a world with fewer languages might be more prosperous and less divided. Others caution that outsiders who campaign to 'save' languages should be careful not to impose their own priorities on communities who may have made a deliberate choice to adopt a new language. These are serious points. Yet they rest on the assumption that people must choose between languages, when the evidence suggests that bilingualism is the historical norm for most of humanity, and that speaking a minority language need not prevent anyone from mastering a majority one.

There are grounds for cautious optimism. Hebrew, which for centuries was used mainly in religious and scholarly contexts, was revived as an everyday spoken language in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries and now has millions of native speakers. Welsh, once in steep decline, has stabilised thanks to bilingual schooling, broadcasting and official status. In New Zealand, Māori 'language nests' — preschools in which young children are surrounded entirely by Māori — have helped produce a new generation of speakers. None of these cases was easy, and each depended on strong commitment from the communities themselves. But they demonstrate that the decline of a language is not an inevitable process: it is the result of human decisions, and human decisions can be changed.
