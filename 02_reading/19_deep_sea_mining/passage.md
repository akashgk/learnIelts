# Treasure on the Ocean Floor

**A**
Scattered across vast areas of the deep ocean floor, four to six kilometres below the surface, lie billions of potato-sized lumps of rock known as polymetallic nodules. They form extremely slowly, growing by just a few millimetres every million years as metals dissolved in seawater build up around a core such as a fragment of shell or a shark's tooth. What makes them commercially interesting is their composition: they are rich in manganese, nickel, cobalt and copper, all of which are used in the batteries that power electric vehicles and store renewable energy.

**B**
The largest known concentration of nodules lies in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, a region of the Pacific Ocean between Hawaii and Mexico covering several million square kilometres. Because this area lies beyond any country's national waters, it is governed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA), an organisation established under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. The ISA has granted contracts allowing companies and governments to explore the region, but at the time of writing it had not yet agreed the rules that would allow commercial mining to begin.

**C**
Supporters of deep-sea mining argue that it could ease a looming shortage of critical metals. Demand for battery materials is projected to rise steeply as countries shift away from fossil fuels, and mining these metals on land often involves clearing forests, polluting rivers and, in some regions, serious human rights abuses. Collecting nodules, they suggest, would require no blasting or digging: vehicles would simply move across the seabed, gathering nodules and pumping them up a pipe to a ship on the surface.

**D**
Many marine scientists, however, are deeply concerned. The deep seabed was once thought to be an almost lifeless desert, but expeditions to the Clarion-Clipperton Zone have revealed a surprising diversity of life, much of it unknown to science. One major survey found that the large majority of species collected there had never been described before. Many of these animals live attached to the nodules themselves, which are the only hard surface available in a landscape of soft mud. Removing the nodules would therefore remove their habitat — and because nodules take millions of years to form, it would not grow back on any human timescale.

**E**
The effects could extend well beyond the mined areas. Collector vehicles would stir up plumes of fine sediment that could drift for long distances and settle on animals that feed by filtering water. Waste water and sediment released from surface ships could affect life in the middle depths of the ocean. A small-scale experiment conducted off the coast of Peru in 1989, in which a section of seabed was ploughed to simulate mining, offers a warning: when scientists returned decades later, the tracks were still clearly visible and many animal populations had not recovered.

**F**
In 2023, scientists also reported that nodules may produce small amounts of oxygen on the dark seabed, a finding that, if confirmed, would challenge the long-held assumption that oxygen in the ocean comes only from photosynthesis near the surface. The discovery has been disputed by some researchers and mining companies, but it illustrates how little is understood about these ecosystems. Partly for this reason, a growing number of governments and major companies, including several car manufacturers, have called for a pause on deep-sea mining until its environmental effects are better understood.
