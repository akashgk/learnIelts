# The Return of the Wolves

**A**
By the 1920s, government-sponsored hunting had eliminated the grey wolf from Yellowstone National Park in the United States. At the time, wolves were regarded as dangerous pests that threatened livestock and reduced the number of deer and elk available to hunters. For the next seventy years, the park's largest predator was absent, and its elk population grew rapidly. Park rangers attempted to control elk numbers by culling, but the practice was unpopular with the public and was abandoned in the late 1960s.

**B**
Attitudes slowly changed. As the science of ecology developed, biologists began to argue that top predators were not simply a nuisance but an essential part of a healthy ecosystem. After two decades of debate, public consultation and legal challenges from farming organisations, 14 wolves captured in Canada were released into Yellowstone in 1995, followed by 17 more in 1996. The animals adapted quickly. Within a decade, the park supported well over a hundred wolves living in more than a dozen packs.

**C**
What happened next has become one of the best-known stories in modern ecology. According to the original account, the wolves did not just reduce the number of elk; they also changed the elk's behaviour. Elk began to avoid valleys and riverbanks where they could easily be trapped, and spent less time grazing in any one place. As a result, young willow and aspen trees, which elk had eaten for decades, began to grow again along rivers. The returning trees stabilised riverbanks, provided shade that cooled the water for fish, and supplied food and building material for beavers, whose dams in turn created new wetland habitats for frogs, birds and insects. Ecologists call such a chain of indirect effects a 'trophic cascade'.

**D**
The story was so appealing that it spread far beyond the scientific community, featuring in documentaries and popular videos watched by millions of people. Yet in recent years a number of researchers have questioned whether it is quite so simple. Some studies found that elk in Yellowstone did not significantly change where they fed, perhaps because wolves hunt mainly at dawn and dusk, while elk can graze safely in risky areas during the middle of the day. Others pointed out that the recovery of willows was patchy: in many places, trees remained short because decades of overgrazing had lowered the water table, leaving roots unable to reach moisture.

**E**
Other factors may also have contributed to the decline in elk. Grizzly bears, whose numbers increased during the same period, are efficient predators of elk calves. Human hunters outside the park boundaries took large numbers of elk that migrated beyond them in winter, and a series of dry years reduced the quality of the animals' food. Disentangling the influence of each factor is extremely difficult because, unlike in a laboratory, there was no 'control' Yellowstone without wolves with which the real park could be compared.

**F**
None of this means that the reintroduction was a failure. Wolves have clearly affected the park, and scavengers such as ravens, eagles and coyotes benefit from the carcasses they leave behind. But the debate illustrates an important point about ecological science: natural systems rarely have a single cause and effect. As one ecologist has put it, 'Wolves are not a magic wand.' Restoring a predator can set changes in motion, but it cannot by itself undo a century of human influence.
