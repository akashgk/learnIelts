# The Trouble with Bees

**A**
Roughly three-quarters of the world's leading food crops depend, at least in part, on animals to carry pollen from one flower to another. Bees are by far the most important of these pollinators. Without them, the supply of apples, almonds, coffee, tomatoes and many other foods would fall sharply, and prices would rise. It is therefore not surprising that reports of declining bee populations have caused widespread concern among farmers, scientists and the public.

**B**
Most people picture the honeybee when they think of bees, but it is only one of more than 20,000 known species. Honeybees are unusual in that they live in large colonies that can be managed by beekeepers and moved from farm to farm. The majority of bee species, by contrast, are wild and solitary: each female builds her own small nest, often in the ground or in hollow plant stems, and raises her young without the help of a colony. Research suggests that these wild bees are responsible for a much larger share of crop pollination than was once believed.

**C**
Since the early 2000s, beekeepers in North America and Europe have reported unusually high losses of honeybee colonies over winter. In some years, a third or more of all colonies have died. Yet the total number of managed honeybee colonies in the world has actually risen over the same period, because beekeepers have replaced lost colonies by splitting healthy ones. The situation for wild bees is more worrying, since nobody is available to replace them, and long-term surveys in several countries show that many species have become rarer or disappeared from large parts of their former range.

**D**
Scientists generally agree that there is no single cause of the decline. The loss of wildflower meadows and hedgerows to intensive agriculture and urban development has reduced the food and nesting sites available to bees. Certain pesticides, particularly a group known as neonicotinoids, have been shown in laboratory and field studies to affect bees' ability to navigate and reproduce, which led the European Union to ban their outdoor use in 2018. Parasites and diseases also play a major role; a mite called *Varroa destructor*, which spread around the world during the twentieth century, weakens honeybees and transmits viruses between them. Climate change adds a further pressure by altering the timing of flowering, so that some plants now bloom before the bees that depend on them have emerged.

**E**
These factors rarely act alone. A bee that is poorly fed because there are few flowers nearby is likely to be more vulnerable to disease, and one that has been exposed to low levels of pesticide may be less able to find food. This combination of stresses makes the problem difficult to study, as well as difficult to solve.

**F**
Nevertheless, there are encouraging signs. Many farmers now leave strips of wildflowers along the edges of their fields, a measure that studies have found increases both the number of wild bees and crop yields in neighbouring fields. Cities, too, can provide valuable habitats: parks, gardens and even roadside verges that are mown less frequently can support a surprising variety of species. Some experts argue that the enthusiasm for keeping honeybees in cities may be misplaced, however, as large numbers of hives can compete with wild bees for the limited flowers available.
